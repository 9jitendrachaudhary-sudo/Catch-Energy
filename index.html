Common questions

Absorption chillers & heat recovery — answered.

What is an absorption chiller and how does it work?
An absorption chiller produces chilled water using heat as the driving energy instead of an electric compressor. It uses a lithium bromide (LiBr) and water solution under deep vacuum: heat boils refrigerant water out of the solution in the generator, it condenses, then evaporates at very low pressure to create the cooling effect, and the lithium bromide re-absorbs the vapour. Because the "compression" is done thermally, the machine draws only a few kilowatts of electricity for its pumps.
Who services absorption chillers in the UK?
Catch Energy is an independent, OEM-trained absorption chiller service provider covering the UK and Europe. We handle planned maintenance, LiBr solution analysis, vacuum and leak testing, commissioning and 24/7 emergency breakdown support for single and double-effect machines driven by hot water, steam or exhaust gas.
What's the difference between an absorption chiller and an absorption heat pump?
They share the same LiBr/water cycle but are used differently. An absorption chiller's useful output is chilled water, with heat rejected to a cooling tower. An absorption heat pump deliberately harvests that heat and delivers it to a heating circuit — recovering low-grade waste heat and upgrading it to useful temperatures for district heating or process use.
How often should an absorption chiller be serviced?
At least annually — including a vacuum decay test, purge system check, LiBr sampling and analysis, approach-temperature survey and controls calibration. Mission-critical machines often use quarterly visits with remote monitoring. Well-maintained absorption chillers routinely run 25–30 years.
Why choose absorption cooling for a data centre?
Data centres turn electricity into heat, then spend more electricity removing it. Where on-site generation or a heat network provides driving heat, absorption chillers produce cooling with a fraction of the electrical demand — freeing capacity for IT load, improving PUE, and using water as the refrigerant with no F-gas compliance burden.
